\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

A standard lesson from models with multidimensional product differentiation is that firms may differentiate strongly along one dimension while remaining close along another. In an influential contribution, \citet{Economides1989} introduces quality choice into a Hotelling duopoly and reports maximal variety differentiation together with minimal quality differentiation. In the three-stage version of the model---location first, quality second, and price last---the paper reports a unique pure-strategy perfect equilibrium over a parameter interval in which firms locate at opposite endpoints and choose the same quality.

This paper revisits the equilibrium logic of that three-stage game. The key point is simple. At the maximal locations, the downstream price game is not described globally by a single smooth formula. When the quality gap is moderate, both firms serve positive market shares and the familiar interior price solution applies. When the quality gap becomes sufficiently large, however, the price equilibrium switches to an exclusion branch in which the high-quality firm serves the market and the rival charges zero. Because quality is chosen before price, a firm can use a finite quality deviation to move the subsequent game from one pricing regime to another. A first-order condition obtained after substituting only the interior price formula therefore need not identify a global quality best response.

We keep the original noncooperative pricing game and derive the maximal-location price continuation over the full quality-gap domain. Writing $\delta=a_1-a_2$, the shared-market prices are
\[
 p_1=1+\frac{\delta}{3},
 \qquad
 p_2=1-\frac{\delta}{3}
\]
for $|\delta|\leq3$, while quality gaps beyond those boundaries induce exclusion. This piecewise continuation generates the main correction. The symmetric quality profile reported by \citet{Economides1989},
\[
 a_1=a_2=\frac1{3\lambda},
\]
is not a Nash equilibrium of the reduced quality game at the maximal locations for
\[
 \frac19<\lambda<\frac4{27}.
\]
A unilateral deviation to $a_i=1/\lambda$ crosses the downstream price-regime boundary and raises normalized profit by
\[
 \frac{4-27\lambda}{18\lambda}.
\]
At $\lambda=4/27$ the two actions tie, and within the original interval the symmetric profile survives as a quality-subgame equilibrium only for $\lambda\geq4/27$.

The same continuation also reveals multiplicity. For every $\lambda\in(1/9,2/9)$, the maximal-location quality subgame admits the two asymmetric exclusion equilibria
\[
 \left(\frac1\lambda,0\right)
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \left(0,\frac1\lambda\right).
\]
Hence the problem is not exhausted by changing a lower parameter bound. Below $4/27$, the reported symmetric quality profile is not an equilibrium; above that threshold, it coexists with at least two asymmetric equilibria. We do not claim that these profiles exhaust the pure quality-equilibrium set. The result needed for the reassessment is weaker and sharper: the reported symmetric quality-stage uniqueness characterization at the maximal locations does not survive global incentive checks.

Welfare comparisons give the multiplicity economic content. Let $S$ denote the reported symmetric profile and $E$ either certified asymmetric exclusion profile. Throughout the original parameter interval,
\[
 \frac{W_E-W_S}{\mu}=\frac{10-9\lambda}{36\lambda}>0.
\]
For $\lambda\in[4/27,2/9)$, where both $S$ and the certified exclusion profiles are quality-subgame equilibria, the exclusion equilibria therefore yield higher total welfare. The consumer-surplus ranking changes at $\lambda=4/21$: below that point the exclusion profile has lower consumer surplus, at the threshold consumer surplus is equal, and above it the exclusion profile has higher consumer surplus as well as higher producer surplus and total welfare. A fixed-location planner chooses the same one-firm allocation and quality as the certified exclusion profiles. The remaining welfare loss relative to first best comes from the active firm being located at an endpoint rather than at the market center.

There is a separate equilibrium-concept issue. Linear Hotelling price competition can fail to possess a pure price equilibrium for feasible histories, as emphasized by \citet{DAspremontGabszewiczThisse1979}. The original paper itself operates in that environment. Under the standard modern full-history definition of pure-strategy subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium, one feasible price subgame without a pure Nash equilibrium is enough to rule out a pure-strategy SPNE for the full three-stage game. We provide a direct witness in Appendix~\ref{app:no-price-ne}. We treat this point cautiously: it is a statement about the modern SPNE interpretation, not a claim about the exact historical meaning intended by the phrase ``perfect equilibrium'' in 1989. More importantly, the global quality-deviation and multiplicity results do not depend on making that terminological point. They arise even when the maximal-location continuation used in the original backward-induction argument is taken as the object to be reassessed.

The closest prior critique is \citet{Bunte1995} and \citet{Bunte1997}. Bunte's critique is direct: he argues that Economides does not impose a stability condition on the R\&D decision and that imposing such a condition changes the differentiation conclusion. Bunte also analyzes alternative resolutions of Hotelling price instability, including a Hotelling scenario based on mutual dependence and a limit-price scenario. Accordingly, this paper does not claim to be the first to identify a stability problem with \citet{Economides1989}. Bunte also cites \citet{Sorenson1995} for the R\&D stability condition; Bunte reports that Sorenson uses the same R\&D specification and does not analyze variety, while the publisher abstract describes product-improving R\&D before differentiated Bertrand competition. Our comparison with Sorenson is limited to that abstract and Bunte's discussion, so it does not establish the absence of equivalent results in the full article. No equivalent result has been identified in the materials examined, exact overlap remains unresolved, and we make no priority claim. The contribution claimed here is therefore only the set of original-game results established below: the piecewise maximal-location price continuation, the exact $4/27$ global-deviation threshold, and the two explicit asymmetric exclusion quality equilibria.

A recent structural comparison is \citet{CohenHeifetz2024}, who revisit spatial competition with exogenous quality asymmetry and quadratic transportation costs in a two-stage location--price game. Their quality gap is exogenous and affects equilibrium locations, whereas the present paper keeps the linear-transport three-stage game of \citet{Economides1989}, makes quality endogenous, and asks whether quality deviations are globally incentive compatible once the subsequent price game is solved on all branches. Their analysis therefore does not deliver the $4/27$ threshold or the asymmetric quality equilibria studied here, but it confirms that the interaction between horizontal differentiation, vertical quality differences, and exclusion remains an active industrial-organization question.

The paper is also related to work combining horizontal and vertical differentiation, including \citet{NevenThisse1990}, the circular quality model of \citet{Economides1993}, attribute-dependent quality provision in \citet{DegryseIrmen2001}, and the location--R\&D--price model with endogenous spillovers in \citet{PigaPoyagoTheotoky2005}. Those contributions reinforce the broader point that horizontal position and quality interact strategically. The narrower lesson here is about sequential games with regime-dependent continuations: when an upstream action can move the downstream game across an exclusion boundary, local optimization on a regular branch is not a substitute for a global equilibrium check.
